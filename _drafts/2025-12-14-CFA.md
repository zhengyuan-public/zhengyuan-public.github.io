### Introduction

Economics is the study of **Production**, **Distribution**, and **Consumption**. There are mainly two areas of study:

- **Macroeconomics**: the study of aggregated economic quantities, such as national output and national income.

- **Micoreconomics**: the study of individual economic units, including consumer and business.

There are key assumptions/theories

- **The theory of the consumer**: utility-maximizing individuals
- **The theory of the firm**: profit-maximizing firms

### Demand Analysis of Consumer

#### Demand Concepts

**The Law of Demand**

Buyers will choose to buy less of good X as the price of it rises and vice versa.

**Demand Function**
$$
Q_x^d = f(P_x, I, P_y) \nonumber
$$

where $$ Q_x^d $$ is the quantitiy demanded of some good X; $$ P_x $$ is the price per unit of good X; $$ I $$ is consumer's income; and $$ P_y $$ is the price of another good Y.

Factors concerning the demand side:

1. Elasticities;
2. Substitutions;
3. Income effects;
4. Normal and inferior goods.

**Two prices**

1. Own Price: the price of the good itself influence the demand of that good.

2. Cross Price: the price of other goods influence the demand of that good.

#### Own-Price Elasticity of Demand

**Elasticity: the ratio of percentage changes**
$$
\begin{align}
E_{p_x}^d = \frac{\%\Delta Q_x^d}{\%\Delta P_x} = \underbrace{\frac{\Delta Q_x}{\Delta P_x}}_{constant} \times \frac{P}{Q} = 
	\begin{cases}
    <1, \text{ inelastic} \\
    =1, \text{ unit(ary) elastic} \\
    >1, \text{ elstic}
	\end{cases} \nonumber
\end{align}
$$

- Elasticity is a measure of how sensitive one variable is to change in the value of another variable.
- Own-price elasticity is typically negative.

**Predicting Demand Elasticity**

1. The portion of the typical budge. (The smaller the portion, the less elastic. e.g., toothpaste)
2. The amount of time available for responding to change in price. (The shorter, the less elastic. Such as gasoline in the short term, because the way of transportation is locked in short-term.)
3. The good is necessary of optional. (The more necessary, the less elastic.)

#### Income Elasticity of Demand

$$
\begin{align}
E_{I}^d = \frac{\%\Delta Q_x^d}{\%\Delta I} \nonumber
\end{align}
$$

- Income elasticity can be positive, negative or zero.

#### Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand

$$
\begin{align}
E_{I}^d = \frac{\%\Delta Q_x^d}{\%\Delta P_y} \nonumber
\end{align}
$$

- The same form as own-price elasticity, except $$ y $$ indicates other goods, a.k.a., **substitutes**
- If the cross-price elasticity of two goods is **positive**, they are **substitutes**, irrespective of people's oppinion.
- If two goods whose cross-price elasticity of demand is **negative** are said to be **complements**, regardless of people's oppinion.

#### Substitution and Income Effects

- The substitution effect and the income effect are two major factors behind the law of demand.

- **Real income** refers to the income of an individual, group, or nation after adjusting for inflation.

#### Normal and Inferior Goods

**Normal goods**

- An **increase** in income would cause consumers to buy **more**.

**Inferior goods**

- An **increase** in income would cause consumers to buy **less**. 
- “Inferior” does not imply anything at all about the quality of the good.
- The same good could be normal for some consumers while it is inferior for others.

|              | Substitution Effect | Income Effect |
| :----------: | :-----------------: | :-----------: |
|  **Normal**  |        More         |     More      |
| **Inferior** |        More         |     Less      |

**Exceptions**

Income effect can be so strong that it can overpower the substitution effect.

- Giffen goods: a decrease in price will cause a decline in demand (such as the demand for rice for people living in subsistence levels, a decline of rice price will enabled them to shift more of their consumption from rice to other food, e.g., meat).
- Veblen goods: an increase in price will cause an increase in demand (such as jewlary because people derive value from being a symbol of the purchaser’s high status in society.)

## Derivatives

### Fundamental Concepts

The underlying assets for equity, fixed-income, currency, and commodity are said to trade in **cash/spot markets**, and their prices are referred to as **cash/spot prices**, though we usually just refer to them as stock prices, bond prices, exchange rates, and commodity prices. **Derivatives** are financial instruments that derive their values from the performance of these basic assets.

It's typically *understood* or *implicit* that:

- **Mutual funds** and **exchange-traded funds (ETFs)** simply *pass through* the returns of their underlying securities.
- **Derivatives** usually *transform* the performance of the underlying asset before paying it out in the derivatives transaction.
- **Derivatives** take their value and certain other characteristics from the underlying asset.
- **Underlying asset** is often simply referred to as the **underlying**, whose value is the source of risk. 

Derivatives are similar to insurance in that both allow for the transfer of risk from one party to another. The risk itself does not change, but the party bearing it does. Like insurance, derivatives pay off on the basis of a source of risk, which is often, but not always, the value of an underlying asset. And like insurance, derivatives have a definite life span and expire on a specified date.

Derivatives are created in the form of **legal contracts** between two parties, the buyer and seller/writer. The buyer of a derivative is referred to as the **long** or **holder** because they benefit from an increase in the value of the instrument, whereas the seller is referred to as the **short** because they benefit from a decrease in its value

### Purpose of Derivatives

1. Derivative markets can improve the performance of the markets for the underlyings.
2. Derivative markets create beneficial opportunities that do not exist in their absence. 
3. Derivatives can be used to create strategies that cannot be implemented with the underlyings alone.
4. Derivatives allow market participants to practice more effective risk management.

### Characteristics of Derivatives

- Relatively high degree of leverage
- Lower transaction costs
- More liquid than their underlyings
- Offer a simple, effective, and low-cost way to transfer risk
  - Market exposure
  - Adjust risks taken

### Risk Management

Risk management is the process by which an organization or individual defines the level of risk it wishes to take, measures the level of risk it is taking, and adjusts the latter to equal the former.

Risk management does not guarantee that large losses will not occur. **Risk taking is inherent in all forms of economic activity and life in general.** The possibility of failure is never eliminated.

### The Structure of Derivative Markets

#### Exchange-Traded Derivative Markets

Exchange-traded derivatives are standardized:

- Terms and conditions are precisely specified by the exchange.
- Limited ability to alter those terms.

This standardization of contract terms facilitates: 

1. the creation of **a more liquid market** for derivatives. Through designated market makers, derivatives exchanges guarantee that derivatives can be bought and sold. 
   1. By standardizing contracts,  everyone is looking at the same "product". This creates liquidity because everyone is trading the exact same thing, making it much easier to find a match.
   2. Derivatives exchanges guarantee a market exists. You will always have a counterparty, the market maker, to trade with. But, the exchange does **NOT** guarantee a good price.
   3. The **Cost of Liquidity** is measured by the **Bid-Ask Spread** (the gap between what a buyer is willing to pay and what a seller is willing to accept). Low Bid-Ask Spread means high liquidity and vice versa.
2. the creation of a **clearing** and **settlement** operation.
   1. **Clearing** refers to the process by which the exchange verifies the execution of a transaction and records the participants’ identities.
   2. **Settlement** refers to the related process in which the exchange transfers money from one participant to the other or from a participant to the exchange or vice versa.
   3. Derivatives exchanges clear and settle all contracts **overnight**, whereas most securities exchanges require two business days.
   4. Provides a credit guarantee by requiring a cash deposit, usually called **margin bond** or **performance bond**.
   5. Market makers are often able to **buy and sell almost simultaneously at different prices**, locking in small, short-term profits, which is commonly known as **scalping**.

##### Transparency 

**Full information on all transactions is disclosed** to exchanges and regulatory bodies.

- Transparency means a loss of privacy
- Standardization means a loss of flexibility
- Regulation means a loss of both

#### Over-the-Counter Derivative Markets

OTC derivatives are customized. OTC markets participants are willing to create and trade virtually any type of derivative that can legally exist. The backbone of OTC markets is typically a network of banks, which are members of the International Swaps and Derivatives Association (**ISDA**). Those banks typically act as **dealers**, so these markets are sometimes called **dealer markets**. They **informally** (not obligated) agree to buy and sell various derivatives.

OTC market is not necessarily less liquid than the exchange market. Liquidity is always driven by trading interest, which can be strong or weak in both types of markets.

#### Types of Derivatives

There are two major types of derivatives:

1. Forward Commitments, which **obliges** the parties to engage in a transaction at future date.
2. Contingent Claims, which provide one party the **right** but **NOT obligation**.

### Forward Commitments

Both parties must do their **due diligence** and there is no **recourse**. Either party could renege on his obligation, in response to which the other party could go to court, provided a formal contract exists and is carefully written. In the financial world, such contracts are very carefully written, with legal provisions that guard against fraud and require extensive credit checks.

#### Forward Contracts

> ***Definition***: A forward contract is an **over-the-counter (OTC) derivative** contract in which two parties agree that one party, the buyer, will purchase an underlying asset from the other party, the seller, at a later date at a fixed price (**forward price**) they agree on when the contract is signed.
{: .prompt-tip }

The long and the short are in a **zero-sum game**:

- **Payoffs**
  - The long: $$ S_{T} - F_{0}(T) $$ 
  - The short: $$ -(S_{T} - F_{0}(T)) $$
    - Spot prices: $$ S_{0}, S_{T} $$
    - Forward price: $$ F_{0}(T) $$, a price established at time $$ 0 $$ and applies to a contract expires at time $$ T $$. It is the "no-arbitrage" price that ensures the contract has **zero value** at inception. The value is obtained using the **Cost of Carry** model, representing the spot price adjusted for the net cost of holding the asset until $$ T $$. 

$$
F_0(T) = S_0 \times e^{(r + c - y)T} \nonumber
$$

- **No money changes hands** when the contract is initiated. Forward contracts have zero value at the start and they are neither assets nor liabilities. 
- Forward contracts need not specifically settle by delivery of the underlying asset. Instead, they can be settled by an exchange of cash and these contracts are called **non-deliverable forwards** (NDFs), **cash-settled forwards**, or **contracts for differences**. They have the same economic effect.
- Transaction costs can be disregarded for now.

#### Futures

> ***Definition***: A futures contract is a **standardized** derivative contract created and traded on a futures exchange in which two parties agree that one party, the buyer, will purchase an underlying asset from the other party, the seller, at a later date and at a price agreed on by the two parties when the contract is initiated and in which there is a daily settling of gains and losses and a credit guarantee by the futures exchange through its clearinghouse.
{: .prompt-tip }

1. Futures contracts require the existence of a futures exchange.
2. Futures contracts involve the daily settlement of gains and losses, a process known as marking-to-market, where funds are transferred between accounts based on changes in the contract's price.
3. The credit guarantee provided by the exchange through its clearinghouse ensures that both parties fulfill their obligations.

- **Futures price**: almost the same as forward price.
- **Settlement price**: the clearinghouse determines a weighted average of trades occurring during a very specific window (usually the last 30 to 60 seconds of trading) at the end of the trading session (often called the closing range or closing period).
- **Margin accounts**: accounts used to trade futures
  - Equity margin accounts involve **the extension of credit**. The required margin is typically less than **10%** of the futures price.
  - **Initial margin**: the money is deposited there to support the trade, not as a form of equity. There is no formal loan created as in equity markets. A futures margin is more of a **performance bond** or **good faith deposit**.
  - **Maintenance margin**: the amount of money that each participant must maintain in the account after the trade is initiated, and it is **always significantly lower than the initial margin**.
  - **Margin call**: a request to deposit additional funds to bring the balance up to the maintenance margin or initial margin.
    - If a party chose not to deposit additional funds, it will be requested to close out the contract as soon as possible and it will be responsible for any additional losses.
    - If the losing party cannot pay, the clearinghouse provides a guarantee that it will make up the loss, which it does by maintaining an insurance fund. If that fund were depleted, the clearinghouse could levy a tax on the other market participants, though that has never happened.

  - **Price limit**: establish a band relative to the previous day’s settlement price, within which all trades must occur.
    - Limit up: trade at a price above the upper band.
    - Limit down: trade at a price below the lower band.
    - Locked limit: the market hits these limits and trading stops.
      - Typically, the exchange rules provide for an expansion of the limits the next day.

    - Price limit helps the clearinghouse manage its credit exposure.

- **Open interest**: the number of outstanding contracts at any given time. Each contract counted in the open interest has a long and a corresponding short.
  - In practice, there is nearly always some open interest at expiration, at which time there is a final delivery or settlement.


- **The futures price converges to the spot price at expiration.**
- **Payoffs** 
  
  - The same as forward contracts, but they are achieved on a day-to-day basis.
  - In a forward contact, with the entire payoff made at expiration, a loss by one party can be large enough to trigger a default. Futures contracts settle gains and collect losses daily, the amounts that could be lost upon default are much smaller and naturally give the clearinghouse much greater flexibility to manage the credit risk it assumes.
  
- Futures markets are **highly regulated** at the national level. In addition, futures markets are **far more transparent** than forward markets. Futures prices, volume, and open interest are widely reported and easily obtained.
- **Futures prices of nearby expiring contracts** are often used as **proxies for spot prices**, particularly in decentralized spot markets, such as gold, which trades in spot markets all over the world.

#### Swaps

> ***Definition***: A swap is an **over-the-counter (OTC) derivative** contract in which two parties agree to **exchange a series of cash flows** whereby one party pays a variable series that will be determined by an underlying asset or rate and the other party pays either (1) a variable series determined by a different underlying asset or rate or (2) a fixed series.
{: .prompt-tip }

- Swaps are more like a forward contracts than futures contracts in that they are OTC contracts, so they are privately negotiated and subject to default.

- Swap contracts also contain other terms, such as the identity of the underlying, the relevant payment dates, and the payment procedure.

- The money owed is always based on **the net owed** by one party to the other.

- Swaps are relatively young financial instruments, having been created only in the early 1980s. But the swap is the most widely used derivative. 

##### Fixed-for-floating Interest Rate Swap

  - The interest payments on the loan are tied to a specific floating rate. For a dollar-based loan, that rate has historically been US dollar Libor (London Inter-Bank Offered Rate). The payment is based on (rate from the Libor market on a specified reset date) x (the loan balance) x (a factor reflecting the number of days in the current interest calculation period).
  - Whatever the terms of the loan are, the terms of the swap are typically set to match those of the loan.
  - **Notional principal**: the loan has an actual balance (the amount owed by borrower to creditor), but the swap does not have such a balance owed by one party to the other. Notional principal is the predetermined dollar amount on which the exchanged interest payments in a derivative contract are based.
  - An amortizing loan, which has a declining principal balance, will be matched with a swap with a pre-specified declining notional principal that matches the loan balance.
  - Only one party pays the other, as determined by the net of the greater amount owed minus the lesser amount.

##### Interest Rate Swap

  - Basis swap: exchange floating for floating
    - TED Spread: the difference between Libor and the T-bill rate

  - Overnight indexed swap
    - The floating payment is calculated by "compounding" the daily overnight rates over the life of the swap.

### Contingent Claims

A contingent claim is a derivative in which the outcome or payoff is dependent on the outcome or payoff of an underlying asset. A contingent claim has come to be associated with a ***right***, but ***NOT an obligation***, to make a final payment contingent on the performance of the underlying. The term *contingent claim* has become synonymous with the term *option*. Options are different in that they **limit losses in one direction**.

#### Options

> ***Definition***: An option is a derivative contract in which one party, **the buyer**, **pays** a sum of money (**option premium**) to the other party, the seller or writer, and receives the **right** to either **buy or sell** an underlying asset **at a fixed price** (also called **exercise price**, **strike price**, **strike**, or **striking price**) either on a **specific expiration date** or at **any time** prior to the expiration date.
{: .prompt-tip }

- Rights
  - **Call/Call Option**: the right to **buy** at exercise price $$ X $$
  - **Put/Put Option**: the right to **sell** at exercise price $$ X $$
- Expiration
  - **American-style**: can be exercised early
  - **European-style**: can be exercised only at expiration
  - These terms are NOT associated with the location of there trading.
- **Payoffs**
  - Call option buyer: $$ max(0, S_T - X) $$
    - $$ S_T > X $$: in the money
    - $$ S_T = X $$: at the money
    - $$ S_T < X $$: out of the money
    - Profit = $$ \Pi = max(0, S_T - X) - c_0 $$, where $$ c_0 $$ is the call option premium.
  - Call option seller: $$ -max(0, S_T - X) $$
    - Profit = $$ \Pi = -max(0, S_T - X) + c_0 $$, where $$ c_0 $$ is the call option premium.
  - Put option buyer: $$ max(0, X - S_T) $$
    - $$ S_T < X $$: in the money
    - $$ S_T = X $$: at the money
    - $$ S_T > X $$: out of the money
    - Profit = $$ \Pi = max(0, X - S_T) - p_0 $$, where $$ p_0 $$ is the put option premium.
      - Limited loss
      - Limited gain since underlying value cannot go below zero (not always true)
  - Put option seller: $$ -max(0, X - S_T) $$
    - Profit = $$ \Pi = -max(0, X - S_T) + p_0 $$, where $$ p_0 $$ is the put option premium.

#### Credit Derivatives

In the early 1990s, however, the development of the swaps market led to the creation of derivatives that would hedge credit risk.

> ***Definition***: A credit derivative is a class of derivative contracts between two parties, a credit protection buyer and a credit protection seller, in which the latter provides protection to the former against a specific credit loss.
{: .prompt-tip }

##### Total Return Swap

- The underlying is typically a **bond** or **loan**, in contrast to a stock or stock index.
- **Credit Protection Buyer**: pay seller the **total return** on the underlying bond. The total return consists of **all interest and principal** paid by the borrower plus any **changes in the bond’s market value**.
- **Credit Protection Seller**: pay buyers either a **fixed or a floating rate of interest**.

##### Credit Spread Option

- The underlying is the credit (yield) spread on a bond, which is the difference between the bond’s yield and the yield on a benchmark default-free bond. The credit spread is a reflection of investors’ perception of credit risk.
- Only works for a traded bond that has a quoted price. 
  - The quoted price (often called the clean price) is the price of a bond excluding any accrued interest.
- A call option in which the underlying is the credit spread.

##### Credit-Linked Note (CLN)

- **Credit Protection Buyer**: (typically banks) hold a **bond or loan** that is subject to default risk (the **underlying**/**reference security**), issue and ***sell*** the **Credit-Linked Note (CLN)**. If the underlying defaults, the principal payoff on the CLN is reduced accordingly.

- **Credit Protection Seller**: (typically investors) **pay** cash upfront to ***buy*** the CLN.
  - The investors are exposed to two risks:
    1. **The Reference Risk:** The risk that the underlying loan defaults.
    2. **The Issuer Risk:** The risk that the bank itself defaults.

##### Credit Default Swap (CDS)

The credit default swap has achieved much success by **capturing** many of the **essential features of insurance** while **avoiding** the high degree of consumer **regulations** that are typically associated with traditional insurance products.

> ***Definition***: A credit default swap is a derivative contract between two parties, a credit protection buyer and a credit protection seller, in which the buyer **makes a series of cash payments** to the seller and **receives a promise of compensation** for credit losses resulting from the default of a third party.
{: .prompt-tip }

> **Parties not wanting to bear certain risks can sell them to parties wanting to assume certain risks.**
{: .prompt-tip }
